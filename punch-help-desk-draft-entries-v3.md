## 1. Advertising Fundamentals

*The building blocks — these ideas apply no matter which channel or platform you're using.*

### Buying Models

**CPM (Cost Per Mille — "mille" is Latin for thousand)** — You pay a set price for every 1,000 times your ad shows up, whether or not anyone actually looked at it. It's the standard way display, video, and streaming TV ads are priced — a way to pay for reach, not results.

**CPC (Cost Per Click)** — You only pay when someone clicks. No click, no charge. Most search ads work this way — you're paying for the action, not just the appearance.

**CPA (Cost Per Acquisition)** — You pay per result — a purchase, a lead, a sign-up — not per ad shown or clicked. It's calculated after the campaign runs (total spend ÷ number of results), and it's usually the number a campaign gets judged by.

**CPV (Cost Per View)** — You pay per video view, mostly on YouTube and similar platforms. A "view" is defined by the platform — usually someone watching 30 seconds, or clicking, whichever happens first.

**CPCV (Cost Per Completed View)** — Same idea as CPV, but you only pay if someone watches the whole thing, start to finish. Common with streaming TV and higher-end video ads.

**vCPM (Viewable CPM)** — A stricter version of CPM. It only counts impressions that actually had a real chance of being seen (see Viewability, below) — not just every ad that technically loaded on a page.

**Flat-Rate / Fixed Placement** — One price for one spot — like renting a billboard for a month — no matter how many people actually see or click it.

**PMP (Private Marketplace)** — An invite-only version of the ad auction. Instead of every advertiser competing for a spot, a website or app opens it up to a smaller, hand-picked group first, usually at an agreed price.

**Programmatic Guaranteed** — You and the publisher agree on price and volume ahead of time, just like a traditional ad buy — it just still runs through the same automated buying systems as everything else.

### Core Metrics

**Impressions** — The number of times your ad was shown. Not the number of people who noticed it — just the number of times it appeared on a screen.

**Reach** — The number of different people who saw your ad at least once.

**Frequency** — On average, how many times each of those people saw it. (Reach × Frequency ≈ total impressions.)

**CTR (Click-Through Rate)** — Clicks ÷ impressions — a rough read on how many people who saw the ad actually clicked it.

**CVR (Conversion Rate)** — Of the people who clicked (or sometimes just saw the ad), how many went on to do the thing you wanted — buy, sign up, whatever the goal was.

**Viewability** — Whether your ad had a real chance to be seen — not just loaded somewhere off-screen. Industry standard: at least half the ad visible for one full second (two seconds for video).

**VCR (Video Completion Rate)** — The percentage of people who watched a video ad all the way to the end.

**Engagement Rate** — Likes, comments, and shares, divided by how many people saw the post. Mostly a social-platform measure.

**Share of Voice (SOV)** — Out of all the ad space bought in your category, how much of it is yours versus your competitors'.

### Targeting Concepts

**First-Party Data** — Information you already have because it's yours — your customer list, your website visitors, your email subscribers.

**Third-Party Data** — Information collected by someone else and licensed to you. Getting harder to come by as privacy rules tighten industry-wide.

**Contextual Targeting** — Placing your ad based on the content around it — a cooking ad next to a recipe — rather than based on who's looking at it.

**Behavioral Targeting** — Placing your ad based on what a person has done before — pages visited, things bought — rather than what they're looking at right now.

**Geofencing** — Targeting anyone physically inside a specific area you've drawn on a map, often a tight radius around one location.

**Lookalike / Similar Audiences** — A new group of people a platform builds because they resemble an audience you already have, like your past customers.

**Retargeting / Remarketing** — Showing ads to people who already showed interest — visited your site, added something to a cart — but didn't follow through.

**Frequency Capping** — A limit on how many times one person can see the same ad in a given stretch of time, so it doesn't wear out its welcome.

### Funnel & Strategy Language

**Upper / Mid / Lower Funnel** — Shorthand for where an ad sits in someone's decision-making journey — upper funnel introduces your brand, mid builds interest, lower pushes toward a purchase.

**Awareness / Consideration / Conversion** — The three broad stages most campaigns are planned around — get noticed, get considered, get chosen.

**Brand Lift** — The measurable increase in how many people know, like, or intend to buy from your brand because of a campaign — usually tracked through a survey.

**Incrementality** — The results that only happened because of your campaign — separate from results that would've happened anyway.

**Attribution** — The method used to decide which ad gets "credit" for a sale. Last-click gives all the credit to the final ad someone saw before buying; multi-touch spreads credit across everything they saw along the way.

**A/B Testing** — Running two versions of something — an image, a headline, an audience — against each other to see which performs better.

**Creative Rotation / Fatigue** — Cycling through different ad creative so the same audience doesn't see the exact same ad so many times it stops working (that decline is called "fatigue").

## 2. Programmatic & DSPs

*The machinery behind most digital ad buying — the part that happens automatically, behind the scenes, once you hit go.*

**DSP (Demand-Side Platform)** — Think of it as the "shopping app" advertisers use to buy ad space across thousands of websites and apps at once, instead of calling each one individually. Most of the buying happens automatically, in real time.

**SSP (Supply-Side Platform)** — The flip side of a DSP — the tool publishers (websites, apps) use to offer up their ad space for sale.

**Ad Exchange** — The marketplace where DSPs and SSPs actually meet and transact — the trading floor connecting buyers and sellers.

**RTB (Real-Time Bidding)** — The auction itself — it happens in the split second it takes a webpage to load, with the ad space going to the highest bidder almost instantly.

**Header Bidding** — Lets a publisher offer the same ad space to several exchanges at once, so they compete against each other — generally pushes the price up in the publisher's favor.

**Deal ID** — A code linking a specific buyer to a specific pre-arranged inventory package in a private marketplace deal.

**Open Exchange vs. PMP vs. Programmatic Guaranteed** — Three levels of the same idea, with increasing control (and usually price): open exchange means anyone can bid, PMP means only an invited group can bid, programmatic guaranteed means price and volume are locked in ahead of time.

**Brand Safety vs. Brand Suitability** — Brand safety means keeping your ad away from obviously harmful content (violence, hate speech). Brand suitability goes further — making sure the content actually fits your specific brand, which takes more judgment than a simple rule can capture. *If a client's suitability needs aren't obvious, that's a conversation for your Punch contact, not a guess.*

**Viewability Standards (MRC)** — The Media Rating Council sets the industry's official definition of what counts as a "viewable" ad — the benchmark everyone measures against.

**Ad Fraud / IVT (Invalid Traffic)** — Fake activity — bots, click farms — that inflates your numbers without a real person ever seeing your ad.

**Pixel / Tag** — A small piece of tracking code placed on a website that records what a visitor does there — used for measurement, retargeting, and figuring out if a conversion happened.

**Floor Price** — The lowest amount a publisher is willing to accept for a piece of ad space.

**How-to: What is a DSP, and how does buying through one actually work?** — An agency sets up a campaign inside a DSP — who to target, how much to spend, which ad to show — and the DSP automatically bids on matching ad space across thousands of sites and apps as people browse. Which DSP to use, and how to set it up, is a strategy decision — this just explains the mechanics behind it.

**How-to: DSP landscape overview** — A few of the bigger platforms you might hear mentioned — The Trade Desk, DV360, Amazon DSP — each with different strengths. Picking the right one for your account is a conversation with your Punch contact, not a wiki answer.

## 3. CTV (Connected TV)

*Streaming TV — think Hulu, Peacock, or a smart TV app — bought and measured more like digital than traditional broadcast.*

**CTV vs. OTT vs. Linear TV** — Linear TV is traditional, scheduled broadcast or cable — what's "on" at a set time. OTT ("over-the-top") means any video streamed over the internet instead of through a cable box. CTV is OTT content specifically watched on an actual television screen — a smart TV, Roku, Apple TV, or game console — as opposed to a phone or laptop.

**Addressable TV** — The ability to show two different households two different ads during the exact same TV show, based on data about each household. Traditional TV with a layer of digital-style targeting.

**ACR (Automatic Content Recognition) Data** — Information a smart TV collects about what's actually playing on screen, used to help measure and target CTV ads.

**Household-Level vs. Device-Level Targeting** — CTV usually targets a whole household (one TV, several possible viewers), not one specific device or person — which limits how precise the targeting and measurement can really be.

**VOD (Video on Demand)** — Content you choose to watch whenever you want, instead of at a scheduled time.

**Live vs. On-Demand Inventory** — Ad space during a live event (a game, an awards show) is priced and sold differently than ad space in on-demand shows and movies.

**Publisher-Direct vs. Programmatic CTV** — You can buy CTV ad space directly from a specific streaming app, or buy it programmatically alongside the rest of your digital plan.

**How-to: Why is CTV buying different from traditional TV buying?** — CTV gives you TV's reach and creative impact, combined with digital's targeting and reporting detail. But it also inherits some real TV-sized headaches — it's harder to control how many times one household sees your ad across different apps and devices, and measurement tools are still catching up to what digital advertisers are used to.

## 4. YouTube / Online Video

**TrueView** — YouTube's original skippable ad format — you only pay if someone watches long enough, or clicks on it.

**Skippable vs. Non-Skippable Ads** — Skippable ads let a viewer skip after 5 seconds, and you only pay if they don't. Non-skippable ads have to be watched in full, and are usually priced on a CPM basis.

**Bumper Ads** — Very short (6-second) ads that can't be skipped — built for getting your brand noticed quickly, not for telling a long story.

**Content Targeting vs. Audience Targeting** — Content targeting picks which videos your ad runs on; audience targeting picks which people see it, no matter what they're watching.

**How-to: Overview of YouTube ad formats** — A quick rundown of the main options — skippable in-stream, non-skippable in-stream, bumper, and in-feed ads — each suited to a different goal. Which one is right for your campaign is a conversation with your Punch contact.

**How-to: YouTube brand safety controls, explained** — What YouTube's built-in safety settings (like content exclusions) actually do, explained simply.

## 5. Social (Meta, TikTok, LinkedIn, Pinterest, Snap, X)

*Different apps, but the underlying setup is more similar than you'd think.*

**Account Structure** — Most platforms organize things in layers. On Meta, it's Campaign → Ad Set → Ad. On Google and TikTok, it's generally Campaign → Ad Group → Ad. Budget and targeting usually live in the middle layer; the actual ad creative sits at the bottom.

**Pixel / Conversions API** — The Pixel is a small piece of code that tracks what visitors do on your website. The Conversions API sends that same kind of information a different way — directly from your server — which has become the more reliable option as browser tracking has gotten less dependable.

**Custom Audience vs. Lookalike Audience** — A custom audience is built from your own data — your customer list, your website visitors. A lookalike audience is a brand-new group the platform builds because it resembles that custom audience.

**Boosted Post vs. Paid Ad** — "Boosting" takes an existing post and promotes it with a few simplified settings — quick, but limited. A properly built paid ad gives you full control over targeting, placement, and how much you bid. Easier isn't the same as more effective.

**Algorithm-Driven Optimization (e.g., Advantage+, Smart+)** — Newer campaign types that hand more of the decision-making — targeting, placement, sometimes even which creative to show whom — over to the platform's own automation. You give up some manual control in exchange for (often) better results with less work.

**Basic Creative Specs** — Every platform has its own rules for image size, video length, and text limits — and they change from time to time. Always check current requirements before finalizing an ad; don't rely on what you remember from last time.

**How-to: How to get verified on Meta** — Verification (for a Business Manager or Page) happens through Meta Business Suite — you'll need your legal business name, address, phone number, and a supporting document like a business license or utility bill. Then Meta reviews it. *If it gets stuck, or the requirements seem off, that's worth a quick check-in with your Punch contact rather than guessing at a fix.*

**How-to: How to set up a Meta Business Manager** — Create the account at business.facebook.com, add your business's Page(s) and ad account(s), then assign access to your team. Everything else — verification, the Pixel, ad accounts — gets built on top of this.

**How-to: How to install the Meta Pixel / Conversions API** — In simple terms: the Pixel is a small piece of code added to your website (often through a tag manager) that fires when someone takes a key action. The Conversions API is its behind-the-scenes counterpart, set up through Business Manager. This part is genuinely technical and specific to your website — bring in your dev team and your Punch contact rather than trying to do it from a wiki page.

**How-to: How to set up a TikTok Business Center** — Same basic idea as Meta's Business Manager — one central place to manage ad accounts, creative assets, and team access, set up through TikTok's own portal.

**How-to: How to set up LinkedIn Campaign Manager access** — Requires a LinkedIn Page first, then you assign account access to your team members inside Campaign Manager.

**Excluded from v1 — Political/issue ad advertiser authorization:** The requirements here change depending on the platform, the ad type, and where you're advertising. The answer here should simply be: *"This isn't one-size-fits-all — check in with your Punch contact before moving forward."*

## 6. Search

*Google and Microsoft/Bing ads, mostly — ads that show up because someone typed something into a search bar.*

**Account Structure (Campaign → Ad Group → Keyword)** — Campaigns hold your overall budget and goal. Ad groups organize things by theme. Keywords and the actual ads live inside each ad group.

**Quality Score** — Google's own 1–10 rating of how relevant your ad is, how likely people are to click it, and how good the page it links to is. A higher score can mean a better position and a lower cost.

**Match Types** — "Broad match" shows your ad for a wide, loosely related range of searches. "Phrase match" needs the core meaning of your phrase present. "Exact match" needs something very close to your exact wording. The wider the match, the more searches you show up for — but the less precise it gets.

**Search Network vs. Display Network** — Search ads appear on the results page when someone actually searches for something. Display ads appear on a network of partner websites and apps based on targeting, not a live search.

**Performance Max (PMax)** — One campaign that automatically runs your ads across all of Google's properties at once — Search, Display, YouTube, Gmail, Maps — with a lot of the decision-making handled by automation rather than manual settings.

**Smart Bidding** — Google's automated bidding strategies, which use machine learning to adjust how much you bid in real time, based on how likely each opportunity is to convert.

**SERP (Search Engine Results Page)** — The page you land on after typing a search — where the regular ("organic") results and the paid ads both show up.

**Ad Rank** — The formula that decides where your ad shows up on the page — or whether it shows up at all — combining your bid, your Quality Score, and how well your ad format is expected to perform.

**Negative Keywords** — Search terms you specifically block your ad from showing up for, to keep out traffic that isn't actually relevant to you.

**How-to: Google Ads account structure, explained** — Walks through the campaign → ad group → keyword setup, and why keeping keyword groups tightly themed tends to help both Quality Score and results.

**How-to: Conversion tracking, conceptually** — The basic idea: you tag the action that actually matters to you (a purchase, a form fill) so the platform can measure it and optimize toward getting more of it. Actually setting this up is technical and specific to your website — bring in Punch and a developer for the real implementation.

**How-to: Google Ads vs. Microsoft/Bing Ads — key differences** — The mechanics are similar, but the audience is different — Bing tends to skew a bit older, with a different device mix, generally less competition, generally lower costs, and a handful of features Google has that Bing doesn't (yet).

## 7. Measurement & Report Literacy

*Probably the section you'll use most — the answer to "what does this number in my report actually mean."*

**Spend vs. Delivery/Pacing** — "Spend" is the money that's actually gone out the door so far. "Pacing" tells you whether that spend is on track, running ahead, or falling behind the original plan.

**WoW / MoM / YoY** — Week-over-week, month-over-month, year-over-year — shorthand for comparing a number to the same period right before it.

**Benchmark vs. Index** — A benchmark is a reference point — like the average click rate across an industry. An index shows how you compare to it: an index of 120 means you're 20% above benchmark.

**Data Lag & Attribution Windows** — Numbers aren't final the second they post — a conversion can still get credited to a campaign for days afterward. That's why the most recent few days of data in a report can still change.

**Viewability Rate (in a report)** — The share of your ads that actually had a real chance of being seen (see Viewability, above). A quality check on your delivery, not a measure of how well the campaign performed.

**Brand Lift Study** — A structured, survey-based study — often run directly through the ad platform — measuring whether a campaign actually moved the needle on awareness or how people feel about your brand.

**Incrementality Test** — A test built to isolate what your campaign actually caused, separate from what would've happened anyway — usually done by holding a group of people back from seeing the ads and comparing results.

**"Optimization" (as a report note)** — When you see this in a report, it usually just means someone made a deliberate change — to targeting, budget, bids, or creative — based on what the data was showing. A normal, ongoing part of running a campaign, not a sign that something went wrong.

## 8. Privacy & Data Basics (concepts only)

*The big-picture ideas only. Anything specific to a law or a state is a conversation for your Punch contact or legal team, not this page.*

**Cookies & the Cookieless Shift** — Cookies are small files a browser stores that, among other things, have historically let advertisers track someone's activity across different websites. Browsers and platforms have been steadily phasing these out, pushing the industry toward first-party data instead.

**IDFA / Apple ATT (App Tracking Transparency)** — Apple's rule requiring an app to explicitly ask your permission before it's allowed to track your activity across other apps and websites. IDFA is the specific device identifier that permission unlocks — or blocks.

**First-Party Data Strategy (general concept)** — The industry-wide shift toward brands relying on data they actually own — website behavior, customer records, loyalty programs — as outside data becomes harder to get.

**Consent Management Platform (CMP)** — The tool behind a cookie banner — it manages and keeps a record of what a visitor has agreed to. This explains what it does in general terms, not what any specific law requires it to include.
