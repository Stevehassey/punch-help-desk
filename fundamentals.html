01 — Ad Basics

Advertising Fundamentals

The building blocks — these ideas apply no matter which channel or platform you're using.

Buying Models

CPM (Cost Per Mille — "mille" is Latin for thousand) — You pay a set price for every 1,000 times your ad shows up, whether or not anyone actually looked at it. It's the standard way display, video, and streaming TV ads are priced — a way to pay for reach, not results.

CPC (Cost Per Click) — You only pay when someone clicks. No click, no charge. Most search ads work this way — you're paying for the action, not just the appearance.

CPA (Cost Per Acquisition) — You pay per result — a purchase, a lead, a sign-up — not per ad shown or clicked. It's calculated after the campaign runs (total spend ÷ number of results), and it's usually the number a campaign gets judged by.

CPV (Cost Per View) — You pay per video view, mostly on YouTube and similar platforms. A "view" is defined by the platform — usually someone watching 30 seconds, or clicking, whichever happens first.

CPCV (Cost Per Completed View) — Same idea as CPV, but you only pay if someone watches the whole thing, start to finish. Common with streaming TV and higher-end video ads.

vCPM (Viewable CPM) — A stricter version of CPM. It only counts impressions that actually had a real chance of being seen (see Viewability, below) — not just every ad that technically loaded on a page.

Flat-Rate / Fixed Placement — One price for one spot — like renting a billboard for a month — no matter how many people actually see or click it.

PMP (Private Marketplace) — An invite-only version of the ad auction. Instead of every advertiser competing for a spot, a website or app opens it up to a smaller, hand-picked group first, usually at an agreed price.

Programmatic Guaranteed — You and the publisher agree on price and volume ahead of time, just like a traditional ad buy — it just still runs through the same automated buying systems as everything else.

Core Metrics

Impressions — The number of times your ad was shown. Not the number of people who noticed it — just the number of times it appeared on a screen.

Reach — The number of different people who saw your ad at least once.

Frequency — On average, how many times each of those people saw it. (Reach × Frequency ≈ total impressions.)

CTR (Click-Through Rate) — Clicks ÷ impressions — a rough read on how many people who saw the ad actually clicked it.

CVR (Conversion Rate) — Of the people who clicked (or sometimes just saw the ad), how many went on to do the thing you wanted — buy, sign up, whatever the goal was.

Viewability — Whether your ad had a real chance to be seen — not just loaded somewhere off-screen. Industry standard: at least half the ad visible for one full second (two seconds for video).

VCR (Video Completion Rate) — The percentage of people who watched a video ad all the way to the end.

Engagement Rate — Likes, comments, and shares, divided by how many people saw the post. Mostly a social-platform measure.

Share of Voice (SOV) — Out of all the ad space bought in your category, how much of it is yours versus your competitors'.

Targeting Concepts

A lot of these are standard digital-advertising concepts — but several are restricted or banned outright for political ads on specific platforms (Google's election ads policy, for instance, blocks most audience-based targeting). Treat the definitions below as "what this means," not "what's available to you" — check the Social and Search pages, or your Punch contact, for what's actually usable on a given platform.

First-Party Data — Information you already have because it's yours — a voter file, a donor list, your own website visitors and email subscribers.

Third-Party Data — Information collected by someone else and licensed to you. Getting harder to come by as privacy rules tighten industry-wide, and unavailable for use in several political-ad targeting scenarios regardless.

Contextual Targeting — Placing your ad based on the content around it — a news ad next to a current-events article — rather than based on who's looking at it. Generally the safest targeting method for political ads, since it doesn't depend on audience data.

Behavioral Targeting — Placing your ad based on what a person has done before — pages visited, past donations — rather than what they're looking at right now.

Geofencing — Targeting anyone physically inside a specific area you've drawn on a map, often a tight radius around one location, like a rally or a polling place. Radius targeting like this is specifically disallowed for Google election ads — confirm availability before planning around it.

Lookalike / Similar Audiences — A new group of people a platform builds because they resemble an audience you already have, like your donor list. Currently disabled for political ads on Meta specifically.

Retargeting / Remarketing — Showing ads to people who already showed interest — visited your site, started a donation — but didn't follow through. Not available for Google election ads, where remarketing is explicitly excluded.

Frequency Capping — A limit on how many times one person can see the same ad in a given stretch of time, so it doesn't wear out its welcome.

Funnel & Strategy Language

Upper / Mid / Lower Funnel — Shorthand for where an ad sits in someone's decision-making journey — upper funnel introduces a candidate or issue, mid builds support, lower pushes toward a specific action like voting or donating.

Awareness / Consideration / Conversion — The three broad stages most campaigns are planned around — get noticed, get considered, get someone to act (vote, donate, volunteer).

Brand Lift — In a commercial context this measures brand awareness; in a political one, the same survey-based method is generally used to measure candidate or issue awareness and favorability instead.

Incrementality — The results that only happened because of your campaign — separate from results that would've happened anyway.

Attribution — The method used to decide which ad gets "credit" for an action — a donation, a sign-up. Last-click gives all the credit to the final ad someone saw; multi-touch spreads credit across everything they saw along the way.

A/B Testing — Running two versions of something — an image, a headline, a message — against each other to see which performs better.

Creative Rotation / Fatigue — Cycling through different ad creative so the same audience doesn't see the exact same ad so many times it stops working (that decline is called "fatigue").
